# Experiment science

Not a schema and not a taxonomy to be filled in. A way of reasoning, written
down so the twentieth probe is better designed than the first. Nothing here is
a required field anywhere; where an idea earned a row it already has one.

## The only question

> Given an uncertain opportunity and a consequential next decision, what is the
> smallest, cleanest, lowest-exposure encounter with reality that produces
> enough trustworthy information to make that decision better?

Not *what kind of experiment is this*, not *how fast can we launch*, not *how
many people can we reach*. The design is synthesised from the uncertainty.

## Start from the assumption chain, not from the business

A venture is a chain, and only one link is currently load-bearing:

problem exists → the right customer is identified → it matters enough → the
value event is useful → they recognise it → the person reached can transact →
a viable exchange exists → distribution is clean → fulfilment works →
economics work → it recurs → it retains → rights hold → legal posture holds →
support stays bearable → scale stays bearable → it fits the portfolio.

Ask which unresolved link blocks the next decision, what observation would move
it, and the least-exposure way to get that observation. Do not test willingness
to pay while a fatal rights question is open. Do not automate fulfilment before
the value event is proven.

**A chain has links you are testing and links you are assuming.** Name both.
The first real probe here tested four links fused into one observation and
assumed three — and the cheapest of the assumed ones turned out to be free to
check in public data, which changed the design.

## Dominance

Probe B is dominated by Probe A if A yields substantially the same
decision-relevant information for materially less cash, time, owner attention,
participant burden, reputation, legal exposure, privacy exposure,
infrastructure or irreversible commitment. **Hunt for dominated designs before
running anything.** This is capital discipline applied to learning.

A design is also dominated when a confound it will suffer from could have been
removed beforehand for free. Spending strangers' attention to produce an
ambiguity a public lookup would have prevented is not a cheap experiment; it is
an expensive one that looks cheap.

## Value of information

Before running anything: what decision changes if the result is positive? If
negative? If ambiguous? **If none of the three changes the next action, do not
buy the information.** Weigh the expected change against cash, attention,
participant attention, reputation, legal uncertainty, future obligation and
opportunity cost.

## Do nothing is a real baseline

Every probe competes with waiting. Waiting preserves cash, reputation,
attention, optionality and future information. *Do not experiment yet* is a
legitimate conclusion, and inactivity is not institutional failure.

## The intervention ladder

Ordered by how much reality is disturbed: internal reasoning · simulation ·
synthetic rehearsal · passive observation · public-data analysis · existing-
market observation · public artifact · anonymous organic use · explicit
interest · signup · waitlist · request · conversation · direct contact · trial
· workflow adoption · deposit · purchase · recurring purchase · integration ·
contract · long-term dependency.

Not a score. A prompt: **could something lower answer enough?** Higher rungs
need stronger justification, and the justification is informational, not
impatience.

## The commitment gradient, and why there is no universal ladder

What the participant gives up: attention · time · identity · contact details ·
feedback · reputation · workflow change · integration effort · data ·
refundable money · non-refundable money · recurring payment · purchase order ·
contract · operational dependence.

Significance is contextual. A procurement-approved PO can outweigh a small
consumer payment. Daily workflow adoption can reveal more durable value than a
trivial transaction. Interpret commitment semantically, never by price.

## Actor separation

User · buyer · payer · beneficiary · decision-maker · gatekeeper ·
administrator · procurement authority. These come apart constantly. A payment
failure may mean **the wrong actor was tested**, not that there is no value.

## Reading evidence

For any observation, ask: what can it support, what can it not support, what
confounds it, how does the exchange change its meaning, how does the actor's
role change it, and how fast does it decay?

| Observation | Supports | Does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Survey answer | that the words resonate | that anyone will act |
| Waitlist signup | curiosity, low friction | willingness to pay |
| Quote request | budget-shaped intent | that a price is acceptable |
| Deposit | expected value, refundable | experienced value |
| Payment before delivery | willingness to pay sight unseen | that the thing is good |
| Voluntary payment after value | that the thing was good | that it can be sold cold |
| Repeat purchase | durability | scale |
| Renewal | durability at that price | expansion |
| Referral | advocacy | economics |
| Silence | nothing at all | anything at all |
| Complaint | that the approach was wrong | that the product is wrong |

**Behaviour outranks praise.** A compliment is not a commitment. And silence is
the most over-read observation there is: it is compatible with every
explanation, which is why probes should be designed so that silence is not the
only possible outcome.

## Exchange changes what you learn

| Exchange | Reveals | Confounds / limits |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed price upfront | willingness to pay sight unseen | trust in sender, price, and value are one observation |
| Pay after value | that experienced value was real | free-riding, generosity; says little about cold sale |
| Sample then paid | whether the sample sells the rest | a good sample can be most of the product |
| Deposit | seriousness, refundably | not experienced value |
| Subscription | commitment to recurrence | a harder question; a no is uninformative about the first unit |
| Usage | that value scales with use | needs a working product first |
| Free with a role | an opinion | opinions about paying are the weakest evidence there is |

**Pay-after-value and payment-before-value are different economic facts.**
Never silently promote one into the other. Upfront price is the cleanest
observation of *pre-delivery* willingness to pay — not the only unambiguous
observation a stranger can produce.

## Value is not one thing

Creation (did something useful happen) · perception (did they notice) ·
capture (could we legitimately charge) · payment capability (could this actor
transact) · friction (did the mechanism suppress capture) · recurrence (does
the value event repeat) · durability (would it repeat under normal conditions)
· customer surplus (did they keep enough that the relationship stays
desirable). A result that looks like failure on one axis is often success on
another: *saved 45 minutes, said it was useful, paid $9, refused a
subscription* is strong creation, strong perception, modest capture, weak
recurring durability — not a failed $29 subscription.

## Different uncertainty, different instrument

Worked examples, to show the instrument follows the question rather than habit:

- **Self-serve SaaS.** Bottleneck is usually *does anyone look for this*, not
  *will they pay*. Search-intent observation dominates a landing page, which
  dominates building anything.
- **Data/API product.** Bottleneck is nearly always **rights and freshness**,
  not demand. Test the source contract before the market; a demand result is
  worthless if the data cannot be redistributed.
- **Monitoring / alerting.** The value event is a rare event. Willingness to
  pay cannot be observed on a normal week; run a shadow period and count the
  events that *would* have fired, then ask whether those were worth money.
- **Directory / lead-gen asset.** Two-sided. Buyer interest does not prove
  supplier liquidity. Match by hand before building matching.
- **Productised service.** Concierge first. Automation economics are a separate
  and later hypothesis; proving the value event by hand is cheaper and more
  honest than proving it with software.
- **Two-sided marketplace.** Liquidity is the whole risk. Manual matching for a
  handful of pairs dominates any platform build.
- **Acquired digital asset.** The uncertainty is *what am I inheriting* —
  liabilities, traffic quality, maintenance, transferability. Diligence is the
  experiment; the purchase is the commitment it justifies.

## Sequencing, interference and afterlife

Learn in stages: evidence → bottleneck → cheapest discriminating probe →
observation → updated judgement → next decision. **Do not launch ten
experiments when the first two could make eight of them irrelevant.** Budgets
are ceilings, not targets.

Experiments interact through shared people, shared organisations, one public
identity, sender reputation, search visibility, providers and legal surface.
Three individually reasonable probes can collectively read as spam.

Every probe leaves something: pages, links, records, preferences, data,
provider resources, unfinished fulfilment. Decide the afterlife before starting
— operate, graduate, archive, close, delete, transfer, refund, fulfil. No
zombie infrastructure.

## What learning is worth keeping

Not only *what the market said* but **what the instrument did**: was the probe
discriminating; which observation mattered; which metric was useless; what
confounded it; was participant burden forecast correctly; was it dominated in
hindsight; did the exchange change behaviour unexpectedly; were the stops right;
did it actually produce decision value?

Preserve belief states honestly — supported, contradicted, superseded, stale,
still unknown — so *"we tried that once"* never hardens into doctrine. Evidence
decays at different rates: price behaviour fast, source quality faster, customer
language slowly.
