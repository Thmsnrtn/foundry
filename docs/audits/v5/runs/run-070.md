## Preconditions

Casey is evaluating Foundry for a startup studio that launches and retires products quarterly. Has 5 test companies. Needs to evaluate the retirement flow -- how easy is it to remove a company? Casey's abandonment trigger #2: "Pricing penalizes product churn -- adding/removing companies should not incur sunk costs."

## Transcript

### Step 1: Evaluate company retirement from a team adoption perspective

Casey needs to know: When we kill a product, can we cleanly remove it from Foundry? Is the process fast enough for a studio that retires products quarterly?

**Result:** Assessment follows.

### Step 2: Find the retirement flow

Casey navigates the UI looking for company retirement.

**Codebase finding:** Same as F-061-A. Retirement is in Privacy & Data, labeled "Delete My Data." No product management section with archive/retire/remove options.

**Friction:** For a studio that retires products quarterly, the deletion flow being in "Privacy" settings is a conceptual mismatch. Studio operators think "retire this product" not "delete my privacy data."

**Result:** FRICTION (HIGH) -- Company retirement flow is conceptually misplaced in Privacy settings.

### Step 3: Evaluate the retirement implications

Casey reads the deletion modal and assesses: What data is lost? What is the timeline? Can the studio recover the data if a product is revived?

**Codebase finding:** Deletion is permanent with 30-day delay. No archive/pause. No revival path after 30 days. No way to re-import historical data if a product is restarted.

**Friction:** Casey's evaluation conclusion: "We spin up and kill products quarterly. If retiring a company from Foundry is painful, we'll never adopt." The permanent-deletion-only model is a blocker for studio adoption. Studios need:
1. Archive (stop agents, keep historical data, don't charge)
2. Revive (re-activate a previously archived product)
3. Fast removal (seconds, not 30-day delay)

**Result:** FRICTION (CRITICAL) -- No archive/revive capability. Permanent deletion with 30-day delay is incompatible with studio workflows that cycle products quarterly.

### Step 4: Evaluate cost implications of product churn

Casey calculates: If the studio adds 4 products/year and retires 3, the Investor-Ready tier at $399/month is a flat fee regardless of active company count. This is actually favorable -- no per-company cost penalty for churn.

**Positive finding:** Flat-fee pricing is good for studios with product churn. No per-company billing means no sunk cost on retired products.

**Result:** PASS -- Flat-fee Investor-Ready pricing does not penalize product churn. Casey's abandonment trigger #2 is partially mitigated by pricing structure.

### Step 5: Evaluate what happens to the "slot" after retirement

Casey asks: After deleting a product, can the studio immediately add a new one in its place?

**Codebase finding:** After `scheduleDataDeletion`, the product remains in the database for 30 days. During this time, the product still appears in the product list. If there is a company count limit on the tier, the pending-deletion product may still count against it, blocking new additions.

**Friction:** The 30-day zombie state (F-063-C) means a retired product may block a new product from being added if there is a cap. This is particularly bad for studios.

**Result:** FRICTION (HIGH) -- Zombie products during 30-day deletion period may block new company additions.

### Step 6: Cross-company boundary check

Casey switches between the 5 test companies. All data is correctly scoped.

**Cross-company awareness check:** PASS. No boundary confusion.

**Result:** PASS -- Boundary maintained.
